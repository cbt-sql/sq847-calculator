How this is figured, and what it leaves out

The basic idea. Oklahoma limits how fast a property's taxable value can rise each year. SQ 847 would lower those limits starting in tax year 2027: homesteads from 3% to 1.75%, and other real property from 5% to 4%. The limit only matters where values are rising faster than it. Where home prices are flat or falling, nothing changes.

Why a range? Counties don't publish how much of their taxable real property is homes versus businesses, farms and other land, and the two have different limits. So the low and high numbers show what happens if all of the county's real property is treated one way or the other. The real answer is somewhere between.

What counts. Only the county general fund's share of property tax, with tax rates held where they are today. Personal property and utility property aren't capped, so they're unchanged. Schools, cities and vo-techs are funded from the same property, so their percentage hit is about the same as the "taxable value" figure shown, but their budgets aren't in these dollar amounts.

What it leaves out. New construction, sales that reset values, and any change to tax rates local governments might make in response. It uses each county's recent home-price trend (Zillow, July 2026) for all real property, and assumes taxable values start out at market value. If values already lag the market, the real effect would be larger.

Data. County general fund property-tax revenue for FY 2024-25 from the OSU County Government Training Program's county abstract (built from county budgets and Oklahoma Tax Commission data); property-class shares from the same source; median incomes from HUD's FY2026 income limits; home values and trends from Zillow. Wagoner County is marked "approximate" because its published valuation row couldn't be verified, so its valuation is estimated from tax collections. Oklahoma County's figures use its certified net valuation and property-class shares from the county's 2025-26 Estimate of Needs and FY2025 annual financial report.
